A self-hosted SMS and email marketing tool that runs on your laptop and sends through your own phone. Forked from ampilares/selfhostsim, audited before first run, reachable from anywhere over Tailscale. Every picture here is drawn into the page, so nothing needs to load.

Sendline

200–500 messages a day, SMS and email, from one screen a non-technical person can use — with an HTTP API your other projects can call. No per-message fee, no vendor holding your contact list, nothing exposed to the open internet.

1 · The problem

You want to send 200–500 messages a day. Every existing option either bills you per message and keeps your contact list, or is a script only you can run.

The third column is the only one where the cost of the 400th message is zero and the list is on hardware you own. That is the whole reason to build this.

2 · The whole product

One picture. Your other apps and the web UI both feed one core; the core paces everything out through a phone and an SMTP relay, and writes down what happened.

Five pieces in the core, and only five. Audience decides who. Render turns a template into real text. Queue survives a restart. Pacer is what stops a carrier flagging you. Ledger is what makes a report true a year later.

The name Sendline is a placeholder. It appears in the compose file, the package names and the UI header — one find-and-replace on day one if you want it called something else.

3 · The fork, audited

You asked for this before anything runs. I read the fork for malicious content before executing a single line of it. Verdict: no malware — but one default that would have sent your data to a stranger.

The fork is honest open-source work, not a trojan. But it is built to point at the author’s own hosted API, and the README expects you to notice. Four lines change that.

The rule this sets. Every third-party default that points at a host you do not own is a finding, even in honest code. The fork is not hostile — it is just configured for its author. Re-run this audit on every git pull from upstream, because a dependency bump is the cheapest place to hide a change.

4 · Keep, drop, build

The fork already solves the hard, boring half: getting bytes to a SIM and a delivery receipt back. It knows nothing about marketing. That is the half we write.

Everything in the green column is code you do not have to write or test. Everything in the red column exists only to serve GoHighLevel and is deleted on day one — roughly a third of the repository, including the entire ghl/ service.

Why fork this one. Not for the UI — there isn’t one. For send-bulk-sms, the sms-status writeback, and an Android app that already survives reboots and reports carrier failures. That is four weeks of fiddly work you skip.

5 · Four rules

Lightweight means few features, not loose rules. These four are the ones that are cheap to honour now and impossible to retrofit later.

Rule 1 is the one with legal teeth — unsolicited marketing texts are regulated almost everywhere, and “it was only 400 people” is not a defence. Rule 2 is the one that keeps your SIM from being cut off. Build both in from the first send, not after the first problem.

6 · The network

One laptop, one phone, one tailnet. No port forwarding, no public domain, no TLS certificate to renew.

The laptop runs four containers and holds the only copy of your data. The phone sits on the same wifi with a charger in it. Every other device — your iPhone, a work laptop, anyone you trust — reaches the UI through Tailscale, from anywhere, with no port open to the internet.

Tailscale, in three commands

Your tailnet is the account at console.tailscale.com — sign in with Google as v30429452@gmail.com. The login is an interactive browser flow, so these are yours to run:

# on the laptop that hosts it
brew install tailscale && sudo tailscale up
tailscale ip -4            # the 100.x.y.z you will point everything at

# on the Android phone and every other device
# install the Tailscale app, sign in with the same Google account

# optional, so you can type a name instead of an address
tailscale cert             # or just use http://sendline:3000 via MagicDNS

7 · One campaign, start to finish

Nine steps from your click to a report. Everything that can surprise you happens at step 2 or step 3, on purpose.

Step 3 is the one people skip and regret: 438 selected becomes 412 eligible, and the UI shows you the 26 and why, before you commit. A system that silently drops 26 people is a system you stop trusting the first time you count.

8 · What it remembers

Eight collections. Three of them already exist in the fork; five we add.

Contact carries consent as a first-class field with its source and timestamp — not a boolean someone set once. Send is one row per recipient per campaign holding the rendered text, so a year from now you can prove exactly what a person received.

Store the rendered text, not just the template id. Templates get edited. If you keep only the id, every historical report quietly starts lying the moment you fix a typo. The extra megabytes are the cheapest insurance in the system.

9 · The pacer

The single most important piece of code you will write, and it is about forty lines.

500 messages at 10 a minute is about 50 minutes of sending. A phone that emits 500 texts in four minutes looks exactly like a spam bot to a carrier, and the punishment is your SIM. Slow is the feature.

At your volume the numbers are comfortable: 500 a day is 15,000 a month on one SIM and one handset. The ceiling you will hit first is the carrier’s daily tolerance, not anything in this software — so make the cap easy to lower and add a second phone before you raise it.

10 · Consent and STOP

The part that is boring to build and expensive to omit.

Three doors in, one door out, and the exit is honoured within seconds because the phone forwards inbound SMS as they arrive. Suppression outranks every other table in the system: no import, no manual edit and no campaign can override it.

11 · The API for your other apps

This is what makes it attachable. Retail-POS, Lunaland, a contact form — one POST and an API key.

Two lanes matter more than they look. An order confirmation that waits 40 minutes behind a promo blast is a broken product; a promo that skips a consent check is a legal problem. Same gateway, same SIM, different rules.

Swagger UI comes free with the fork at http://100.x.y.z:3001/, so the docs for this are generated, not written.

12 · The screens

Eight screens. If a ninth is needed, something else is wrong.

The test for every screen: could someone who has never heard of an API use it without you standing behind them? That rules out jargon in labels, raw JSON anywhere, and any number shown without the word that explains it.

13 · Dashboard

Answers four questions in one glance: is the phone alive, how much have I sent today, what is running, and did anything break.

“Phone online · 112 of 500 sent today’” in the top-right is the single most valuable element on the page. The failure mode of this whole system is a phone that quietly went offline, so it gets permanent screen real estate.

14 · Contacts

A spreadsheet that happens to know who agreed to be contacted.

Consent is a visible column, not a hidden flag. A row marked STOP or no consent stays searchable — you can still see the person and their history — but the UI will not let you put them in a send. Making the rule visible is what stops someone working around it.

15 · Compose

Three steps: who, what, when. The right-hand pane is the whole design.

Nobody should press Send while wondering what will actually arrive. The right pane shows the first real recipient’s real message, the true audience count, the people held back and why, and how long it will take. The STOP line is appended automatically, because remembering it is not a skill anyone should need.

The one non-obvious detail. Show the character count and the segment count live. 160 characters is one SMS; 161 is two, and an emoji silently drops the limit to 70. On a phone-based gateway that is not a cost — it is double the sending time and double the carrier exposure.

16 · The report

438 picked, 412 sent, 406 delivered. A report is only useful if it explains the gaps.

Every number here is read from the Send rows, never recomputed from a template or a list — which is why it still reads the same next year. The failure table names the carrier reason and the next action, because “4 failed” on its own is not information.

17 · On a phone

The same web app, added to the home screen. No app store, no second codebase.

A manifest.json, an icon and a service worker — about an hour of work — and the URL installs like an app on iOS and Android. Over Tailscale it works from anywhere, which is the point: you should be able to pause a bad send from a bus.

18 · Build order

Ordered so that something works at the end of every day, and so the first real message leaves the building on day two.

The order is deliberate: the risky, unfamiliar parts — the phone, the APK, the carrier — come first, while there is still time to discover a dead end. The UI comes after the engine, because a pretty screen over a broken sender is the worst possible place to be on day six.

So when can you start using it

Day 2 — Tue 27 Oct

A real SMS leaves your SIM, triggered by an HTTP call. No UI. Enough to prove the whole risky half of the system works.

Day 7 — Tue 3 Nov

You run real SMS campaigns from a real UI, paced and logged. This is the date that matters — from here it is in daily use while you build the rest.

Day 12 — Tue 10 Nov

v1: SMS and email, scheduling, consent, reports, an API your other apps can call, installable on a phone.

If it is evenings only

Twelve working days at three evenings a week is about four calendar weeks — first campaign in the UI around mid-November. The work does not grow; only the calendar does.

What would actually make this slip. Not the code — the Android build. A Gradle 7.2 project with a 2022 Firebase BOM on a current JDK is the single most likely place to lose a day. Do it on day two, not day ten, and if it fights you for more than two hours, go straight to phase-2 polling and delete Firebase entirely.

19 · What comes after

Deliberately not in v1. Each one is cheap once the ledger and the pacer exist.

LaterWhy it waits
LATER A second phone, round-robinOne SIM covers 500 a day comfortably. Add the second when the cap, not the software, is the limit.
LATER Phone polls instead of FCMDeletes the last third party. Worth a weekend once everything else is stable.
LATER Email open and click trackingNeeds a pixel and link rewriting, which means your own reachable URL. Real work, small payoff at this size.
LATER Drip sequences and automations“Three days after signup, send X.” Easy once campaigns and the scheduler exist; confusing to design before.
LATER A/B subject and body testsNeeds volume to mean anything. At 400 a day the result is noise.
LATER Two-way conversation threadsThe inbox covers replies. Full threading is a different product.
NEVER Multi-tenant accounts and billingThe fork’s subaccounts module and its payments SDK get deleted. This is one person’s tool; tenancy is the complexity that eats projects like this.
NEVER Buying or scraping contact listsRule 1. A purchased list is how a personal SIM becomes a blocked SIM, and how a small tool becomes a legal problem.
NEVER Hosting it on the public internetNo port forward, no public domain. Tailscale is not a temporary shortcut here — it is the security model.

One thing to set up on day one and never think about again: docker run --rm -v sendline_data:/d -v ~/Backups:/b alpine tar czf /b/sendline-$(date +%F).tgz /d on a weekly cron. The whole system is one volume. Losing it loses every contact and every consent record you have, and consent is the one thing you cannot reconstruct.
